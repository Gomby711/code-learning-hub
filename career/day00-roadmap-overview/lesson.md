# Day 0 — The Roadmap: What "Job-Ready" Actually Requires

## Objectives
- Get an honest, concrete picture of the gap between "finished a course" and "hired to write code"
- Understand the four separate skills that together make up "employable developer," so you can tell which one
  you're weak in instead of vaguely feeling behind
- Know what the rest of this track covers, and — just as importantly — what it deliberately does *not* cover
  (because no single course can, and pretending otherwise wastes your time)

## The uncomfortable truth up front

If you finish all 14 days of the Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, and HTML/CSS tracks, you will be able to
**read, write, and reason about real code** — that is a genuine, substantial skill, and it is not nothing. But
by itself, it does not get you hired. Companies don't hire "someone who can write a `for` loop correctly" —
thousands of people can do that. They hire people who can (1) build something that works, (2) collaborate on
code with other people without causing chaos, (3) reason through unfamiliar problems out loud under mild
pressure, and (4) prove all three of the above with evidence a stranger can check in five minutes. Those are
four distinct, learnable skills, and this track exists to build the three you haven't touched yet.

## The four skills, named honestly

1. **Language fluency** — can you write correct, reasonably idiomatic code in at least one language, and read
   someone else's? *This is what the Python / JS-TS / HTML-CSS tracks build.* You get here by finishing those
   and then writing more code beyond their exercises — fluency comes from volume, not from finishing a syllabus.
2. **Tooling & collaboration fluency** — Git and GitHub specifically. Nobody at a job hands you a finished
   folder to edit alone forever; you branch, you open pull requests, other people comment on your code, you
   resolve conflicts. This is almost never taught inside "learn X language" courses, and it shows immediately
   in how a new hire behaves in their first week. **Day 1** of this track.
3. **Problem-solving under a structured format** — technical interviews (LeetCode-style questions, whiteboard
   or shared-doc coding, "walk me through your thinking") test a different skill than "can you build an app":
   can you recognize a problem's shape (sorting? hashing? recursion?), reason about efficiency, and communicate
   your approach clearly while under time pressure? This is trainable, separately from language fluency, through
   deliberate practice on a fairly small set of recurring patterns. **Days 2-3.**
4. **Proof of ability to build something real, alone, and ship it** — a portfolio isn't a nice-to-have
   decoration; it is the single strongest signal you can offer a stranger who has never met you and has 200
   other resumes to get through. A live link beats a paragraph describing your skills every time. **Days 4-7**
   cover planning a real project, understanding the modern stack well enough to build a full one (frontend +
   backend + database), and actually deploying it somewhere a stranger can open.

Day 8 pulls all four together into the actual job-search process: resume, portfolio presentation, behavioral
interviews, technical interviews, and a realistic timeline for how long this genuinely takes.

## How to use this track relative to the language courses

You do not need to *finish* Python, JS/TS, and HTML/CSS before starting this one — Day 1 (Git) and Days 2-3
(algorithms) are useful the moment you can write basic functions and loops, which happens around Day 2-3 of any
of the language tracks. A sensible order, if you're doing this for the first time:

1. Get through roughly the first week of one language track (through its "mini project" day).
2. Start interleaving this track's Day 1 (Git) alongside it — from that point on, actually use Git for every
   exercise file you touch, in a real repository, not just in the abstract.
3. Once you finish a language track's Week 2, do Days 2-3 here seriously — you now have enough language fluency
   to actually implement the algorithm patterns instead of getting stuck on syntax while learning them.
4. Days 4-8 are the finishing stretch, ideally done once you've completed at least one full language track.

## What this track does *not* do

It will not write your portfolio project for you, land you interviews, or replace the hundreds of hours of
independent practice that separate "finished the lessons" from "fluent." What it does is remove the two biggest
categories of "nobody told me this was even a thing I needed to learn" — Git/GitHub workflow, and how the pieces
of a real, deployed, full-stack application fit together — plus give you a structured, honest run at algorithmic
interview prep and the job search process itself.

## Exercises

There's no `exercises.py` for today — Day 0 across every track in this repo is orientation, not a coding
exercise. Your one task: skim the Day index in this folder's `README.md`, and if you're actively job-hunting or
job-hunting soon, read Day 8 (`interview-prep-and-job-search`) *now*, out of order, just once — knowing the
finish line early makes every day between now and then easier to aim correctly.
